Funny poems for kids and grown-ups

Light verse about the comedy of ordinary life: alarms that are ignored, diets that begin tomorrow, homework that hides, and uncles who dance at weddings. All clean, all short, all our own.

English

  • The Alarm

    At five it rang. I shut it off. At ten past five, again. I told it firmly, "Five more, please," And slept right through till ten. My plans were grand the night before: A walk, a book, a run. The only race I won today Was me against the sun. (It won.)

  • The Diet

    On Monday I began my diet: One salad, and some air. By Tuesday I was looking at The fridge with deep despair. On Wednesday, at a wedding feast, I told my plate, "Be small." The plate, I must report with shame, Did not obey at all.

  • The Weighing Machine

    I stepped upon the weighing scale. It gave a little groan. It said, "One person at a time." I said, "I am alone."

  • Password

    My password must have capitals, A number and a sign, The name of my first teacher's cat, And be unlike the last nine. I typed it in. It said, "Too weak." I tried again: "Too long." I now have forty passwords and I know that each is wrong.

  • Wedding Uncle

    At every wedding there is one: An uncle on the floor. His tie is round his forehead now, He's asking for one more. He does the snake, he does the bulb, He's lost a shoe, I think. Tomorrow he'll deny it all. Tonight he's in the pink.

  • The Remote

    The remote is lost. We search the couch, The table and the floor. We blame the dog, we blame the kids, We look behind the door. An hour gone, the show is done, We give the search a rest. And Father stands, and there it is: He sat on it. He's blessed.

  • Group Photo

    "Say cheese!" Somebody blinked. Again. "Say cheese!" The baby cried. "Say cheese!" The tall one blocked the short, And Grandpa stepped outside. On take eleven, all in place, All smiling, all in view, The battery of the phone gave up. We'll meet next year. Adieu.

  • Two Trains

    A train leaves Delhi at ten to nine, Another leaves at three. They ask me when the two will meet. They never think of me. I'd tell them: trains run late, my friend, By hours, give or take. The only two things sure to meet Are me and my mistake.

Why funny poems are harder than they look

A serious poem can limp and still be respected. A funny one has to land. The joke lives in the rhythm and the last line, so these poems are built to be read aloud: a steady beat, a clear rhyme, and a turn at the end. If you are choosing one for a recitation, pick the one that makes you laugh, because the audience can tell.

How to recite a funny poem

  • Keep a straight face. The speaker who laughs first gets fewer laughs.
  • Speak the build-up at normal speed, then slow down for the last two lines.
  • Pause for half a second before the final line. That pause is where the laugh gets ready.
  • If the audience laughs, wait. Do not talk over it. Start again when it fades.
  • Use one prop at most: an alarm clock, a school bag, a plate.

Where to use them

  • Recitation competitions, where one funny poem after five serious ones wins the room.
  • Class parties, Children's Day programmes and talent shows.
  • Family group chats on a slow Sunday.
  • The light part of a farewell or birthday speech.

Who is the joke on?

Every poem here laughs at the speaker, at a situation everyone shares, or at an object such as an alarm clock or a weighing machine. None laughs at a person for how they look, speak, or where they come from. Keep to that rule when you write your own, and your poem will be welcome everywhere. Clean jokes without rhyme are on our sister site, Jokes.

Good to know

  • The best funny poem for a child to recite is one about something the child actually does.
